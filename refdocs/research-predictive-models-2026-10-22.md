# Research — Predictive models & algorithms for a competitive edge (2026-10-22)

> Deep-research pass requested by Azim: which predictive models or algorithms would give Virtual KL an edge across the app, the agent and the KL Twin sandbox. **Candidates, not decisions.** Nothing here is an ADR; the brainstorm is still open. Builds on `virtual-kl-sources.md` and `brainstorm-2026-10-22.md` (Chronos, AgentSociety 2, Groundsource, Aurora are covered there and not repeated).

---

## 0. The short answer

The edge comes less from one big model and more from **five algorithms that nobody else at a hackathon will chain together**, each one cheap and each one backed by published evidence:

1. **A behaviour model with real science behind it.** Residents decide using the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM) structure, with priors from a 49-study evacuation meta-analysis, updated by our survey. Every citizen gets an individual probability, not a stereotype.
2. **Warnings spread through people, not just phones.** Diffusion through household and WhatsApp-group networks, with a "who to tell first" seeding optimiser. This turns the Taman Sri Muda finding (people learned from neighbours) into an operator lever.
3. **The agent remembers that crying wolf has a cost.** Every resident carries a trust state that false alarms erode. The warning threshold is chosen with a cost-loss rule that accounts for lost compliance.
4. **Forecasts with guaranteed honesty.** Chronos-2 / TabPFN-TS forecasts, bias-corrected CAMS and fire-hotspot covariates for haze, all wrapped in **conformal prediction**. That lets us say "our 90% band held 9 times in 10 in the backtest" and prove it.
5. **A living twin.** The haze field between DOE stations is a Gaussian-process (kriging) map with uncertainty, re-fitted on every new reading. It is a twin that tracks reality, not an animation.

Of these, only **two are trained models in the strict sense**: the behaviour model (fitted) and the forecaster/bias-corrector (fine-tuned or fitted). The flood model is **blocked on data** (§3.5). Everything else is an algorithm (no training) that makes the simulation smarter. That keeps the earlier answer of roughly three trained models intact.

## 2. The core five, in detail

### 2.1 Behaviour model: PADM + Bayesian priors + survey (Model C)

**What.** Each synthetic resident's "do I act on this warning?" decision follows the Protective Action Decision Model (Lindell & Perry): receive warning → perceive threat → judge personal impact → check social cues → choose action. Implemented as a **hierarchical Bayesian logistic model**: one model for the whole population, with each agent's own traits as inputs. Every citizen gets an individual probability, which is what "each citizen is simulated" should mean.

**Why it's an edge.**
- The priors come from a real meta-analysis rather than guesses. Huang, Lindell & Prater (2016) pooled **38 actual-response and 11 hypothetical-scenario hurricane studies**. Reliable predictors were **official warnings, living in a risk area, storm and social cues, and expected severe personal impact**. **Demographics had small effects.** That last finding matters: our personas shouldn't lean on age or income stereotypes.
- The survey (D-14) becomes a Bayesian update to those priors, so even ~30 responses move the model honestly. Fewer responses give wider bands, which we show.
- It avoids the "LLM decides everything" trap, which has now been measured. In a 1,000-agent, 10,000-decision social simulation, LLM action selection was on average **563× slower** than a finite-state policy (best case 135×). It also **drifted from the intended behaviour** (mean JSD 0.212; one configuration 0.672), and extra prompt guidance introduced new biases. The authors' conclusion: LLMs are "not a direct replacement for explicit decision policies."
- That supports D-09: Claude writes the personas and explains decisions in interviews; the fitted model decides.

**Gap that is also an opportunity.** No published PADM-structured, survey-calibrated **flood** ABM came up in this search. Closest work: a PADM hurricane ABM in NetLogo, and a 2026 paper individualising a Japanese flood-evacuation ABM from survey data (matching the published group distribution within 1.5 pp). "First survey-calibrated PADM flood twin for a Malaysian township" is a defensible novelty claim, but verify before using it in the pitch.

**Caveat.** The meta-analysis is about hurricanes in the US. Use it as a prior and label it so; the survey moves it toward KL.

### 2.2 Warning diffusion through people + seeding optimiser

**What.** Each household sits in a synthetic social network (neighbours, a WhatsApp group per block or road, a surau or residents' committee hub). When the agent sends a warning, it reaches some people directly; others hear from neighbours with a probability per contact (independent cascade or Bass diffusion). Agents also watch what neighbours *do* (social cues are a top PADM predictor, §2.1).

**Why it's an edge.**
- It models how residents actually found out in Taman Sri Muda: from neighbours and WhatsApp. Most competitors will model the broadcast only.
- It creates a **new operator lever: "who to tell first."** Seeding high-degree nodes beat random seeding significantly in both scenarios tested (AAMAS 2013), and diffusion effectiveness depends on network structure and seeding strategy (Hui et al.). The pitch line: "Tell these 40 people (block captains, the surau committee, condo management) and 80% of the township knows within 30 minutes." The number comes from the simulation and is labelled simulated.
- Haer et al. (2016) found tailored, people-centred messaging can beat top-down government communication **even when it reaches fewer people**, which is exactly our personalised-message thesis.
- Cai et al. (2017): stronger social media makes evacuation more sensitive to broadcast and neighbour signals and **widens the range of outcomes**. That gives us a reason to show outcome bands, not a single number.

**Data.** The network is synthetic and must be labelled so. Tie strength and group sizes are survey questions we could add ("How many WhatsApp groups for your area are you in?").

### 2.3 Trust / cry-wolf state + cost-loss warning threshold

**What.** Every resident carries a **trust score** for Virtual KL warnings. A false alarm lowers it, a correct warning raises it, and trust scales their probability of acting next time. The agent decides when to warn with a **cost-loss rule**: warn when P(event) > threshold, where the threshold is set *above* the naive cost/loss ratio to protect future compliance.

**Why it's an edge.**
- Judges will ask "won't you spam people?" This answers it with a mechanism. Roulston & Smith ("The Boy Who Cried Wolf Revisited") show that when compliance falls with false alarms, **the best warning threshold is higher than the cost–loss ratio**. The benefit is largest when confident forecasts are common and users are moderately intolerant of false alarms.
- Field evidence for the mechanism: a Kyushu (Japan) river-flood study (n = 997) found **perceived** false-alarm rate significantly lowered evacuation probability. The *actual* false-alarm rate did not even predict perception. The effect was weaker for people who felt positive emotions about false alarms. Design implication: an "all clear — thanks for preparing" follow-up message reframes a false alarm as a drill. The agent should send one.
- Counter-evidence to keep us honest: after an unnecessary earthquake early warning in Israel (n = 1,043), the public showed considerable tolerance for false alerts. Trust erosion is hazard-dependent, so the decay rate is a parameter we label, not a law.
- Set thresholds **ex ante from stated preferences**, not tuned after the fact. Ex-post-optimised thresholds are unstable (ECB working paper).

**In the sandbox.** A "replay the season" mode: the same agent with a twitchy vs a calibrated threshold over 30 simulated days, showing compliance at the real event. This is visual and memorable.

### 2.4 Conformal prediction around every forecast

**What.** Take any forecaster's output, measure its errors on a rolling backtest, and widen or narrow the interval so it **achieves the stated coverage** (e.g. 90%). Variants built for time series: split conformal for multi-step forecasts, EnbPI (no exchangeability assumption), SPCI (narrower intervals at target coverage on air-pollution data).

**Why it's an edge.**
- It turns "62% chance API passes 100 by 2 PM" from a claim into a checked claim. Judges score evidence (Team/evidence 10%, Feasibility 20%). A coverage chart from the backtest is the strongest single slide we could have.
- It's model-agnostic: works on Chronos-2, TabPFN-TS or a bias-corrected CAMS forecast without retraining. A 2026 benchmark found multi-step split conformal met the 90% coverage target with the best efficiency (on sales data, so we verify on air quality).

**Data.** Needs a history of forecasts vs outcomes. That starts the day P1 workers begin persisting readings.

### 2.5 Haze: bias-corrected CAMS + forecaster with fire and wind covariates (Model A), on a kriged field

**What, in three parts.**
1. **Bias-correct CAMS.** CAMS global forecasts were weak in Malaysia 2018–2020 (CSI 31%, R² 0.36), poor at local variation, but **better during the 2019 transboundary haze**. The authors explicitly propose bias-correction and early-warning use. A small model (gradient boosting or linear) maps CAMS (via Open-Meteo) to the nearest DOE readings. This is cheap, it's trained, and it fixes the known weakness.
2. **Forecast with covariates.** Chronos-2 led GIFT-Eval at release (79.8% WQL win rate vs TiRex 70.4%, TimesFM-2.5 70.0%), though newer models have since passed it on some metrics. **But** a 2026 controlled study found **TabPFN-TS captures simple covariate–target relationships better than Chronos-2, especially at short horizons**. Haze is exactly that kind of problem: wind direction plus fire hotspots upwind drive PM2.5. Plan: run both, backtest, keep the winner per horizon. Covariates: wind (Open-Meteo), fire-hotspot counts upwind (NASA FIRMS, free key; a Southeast Asia study used hotspot counts lagged up to 31 days as PM predictors), CAMS forecast.
3. **Kriged field.** Interpolate between the 68 DOE stations with universal kriging or a Gaussian process. Universal kriging was the best interpolation method for PM2.5 in Peninsular Malaysia's central and southern zones (2019 and 2020). A GP gives an **uncertainty map**: the twin shows haze *and* where we're unsure (far from stations). With satellite AOD, GP models reached cross-validated R² 0.81 in China (vs 0.74 GWR). That's a Tier 2 upgrade for us.

**Why it's an edge.** Haze in KL is mostly transboundary. A forecaster that "sees" fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan and the wind carrying smoke is explainable to residents ("smoke from fires 400 km south-west, wind turning towards KL tonight"). That is the kind of receipt the brand promises.

## 3. Supporting candidates

### 3.1 Flash flood: what we can do now (Model B stand-in)
- **Rainfall-intensity triggers.** DBKL activates its flash-flood hotspot task force at **22 mm/h**. Recent KL events: **96.5 mm in one hour** at Universiti Malaya (6 May 2026), "far exceeding" JPS warning levels; **114 mm/h** in the city centre (Oct 2024, vs a stated normal of ~60 mm/h). DBKL lists **14 ponding hotspots** (down from 30+). The JPS warning-level number itself was not found.
- **HAND terrain screening** (Height Above Nearest Drainage) from a free DEM marks low-lying cells per development. Cheap; ~76–80% agreement with FEMA maps in North Carolina; errs toward over-estimating extent, which is the safe side for warnings.
- **Fast flood layer for the twin:** cellular-automata models (CADDIES WCA2D / caflood, MIT licence, CPU or GPU via OpenCL) simulate urban pluvial flooding with simple rules instead of shallow-water equations. CA-ffé predicts max depth in seconds to minutes but can't show flood evolution or velocity. Still labelled illustrative (D-10).

### 3.2 Flash flood: the trained model, later (Model B)
- Closest local evidence: a Puchong Jaya flash-flood study (15-min rain + water level, Dec 2023, validated May 2024) found **Random Forest beat LSTM**, with recall 1.0 but accuracy 0.50. Few events means lots of false alarms, which loops back to §2.3.
- An LSTM on Klang River water level (Sulaiman Bridge) reached R² 0.98, but that's river level, not flash floods.
- **Blocker:** no open, downloadable historical JPS rainfall or water-level time series turned up (data.gov.my has annual averages; Sarawak publishes station locations only). **Action: start archiving every JPS and rainfall reading the P1 workers fetch, from day one.** In a few months that history is our training set, with Groundsource and news events as labels.

### 3.3 Simulation-based inference (Tier 2)
Neural posterior estimation (the `sbi` ecosystem; GenSBI in JAX with calibration checks) can fit hidden sandbox parameters (trust decay, contact probability) to observed outcomes, with full uncertainty. Caveats from the literature: it still needs good summary statistics, and it **degrades noticeably when the simulator is misspecified**. Use it once we have real target outcomes (e.g. survey "how did you hear?", Groundsource event timings), not before.

### 3.4 Surrogate emulator + optimisation (Tier 2, Plan mode)
Train a fast model on thousands of our own sandbox runs, then sliders respond instantly and an optimiser can search "best warning time × seeding × message" automatically. A 2020 comparison found **neural networks and gradient-boosted trees beat Gaussian-process emulators in most scenarios**. Use GBMs. This gives the planner a "find the best plan" button, though it is only as good as the simulator.

### 3.5 Ensemble Kalman filter (roadmap)
EnKF-coupled ABMs improved real-time crowd simulation substantially (Grand Central Station, 2024). It needs live observations of the *agents* (crowd counts), which we don't have. The kriged haze field (§2.5) is our practical, cheaper version of a twin that assimilates data.

### 3.6 Scaling the LLM parts (only if needed)
APS (Adaptive Prototype Simulation, 2026) queries the LLM for prototype agents, singleton outliers and shadow-audit agents, then propagates to the rest. It reports a **381.1× call reduction at 10M agents** with JSD 0.094 vs the full-LLM reference. It's only relevant if "interview a resident" or narration ever needs population-wide LLM calls. D-09 already avoids that.

### 3.7 Visual scale (WebGPU)
WebGPU compute shaders run 10,000+ agents at 60 fps in public demos (boids, with WebGL2 fallback), and a million-agent 2D demo exists. Three.js's WebGPU/TSL path supports GPU compute and instanced buffers. This matters for the "thousands of residents" visual (D-12) and is separate from the predictive work.

## 5. Honest gaps found in this research

- No open historical JPS 15-minute series located. Model B waits on our own archive.
- No PADM-structured, survey-calibrated flood ABM found. It's a novelty opportunity, but verify before claiming "first."
- Chronos-2's covariate handling is weaker than its benchmark rank suggests; TabPFN-TS may win on short-horizon haze. Decide by backtest, not by leaderboard.
- CAMS is weak in Malaysia outside haze episodes. Bias-correct before trusting it.
- The meta-analysis priors are US hurricane data. Label as priors; the survey moves them.
- Several items above were read at abstract level only (APS, Chronos-2 covariates paper, Kyushu false-alarm study). Read the full papers before quoting numbers in the pitch.
